# Persistent Organization, Accumulation, and Long-Horizon Viability

## 1. Starting Point

Reality does not provide large organized outcomes as primitive transitions.

A complex organization must arise through physically admissible intermediate states.

This yields a basic structure:

```text
local physical transitions
+
limited rates of change
+
persistent disturbance
+
finite resources
->
problem of preserving and extending organization through time
```

The central question is not:

```text
Why does reality want complexity?
```

Nor is it:

```text
What objective does nature optimize?
```

It is:

```text
Under what conditions can organization persist,
accumulate,
and support increasingly distant outcomes
without being reconstructed from scratch?
```

This is the problem of long-horizon viability through accumulated organization.

---

## 2. No Teleology Is Required

The framework does not require claims such as:

```text
nature wants progress
```

```text
evolution seeks complexity
```

```text
systems try to accumulate
```

Reality need only permit some organized states to persist long enough to influence later transitions.

If a structure survives, later transformations can begin from that structure rather than from a less organized state.

Therefore:

```text
persistence
->
possible reuse
```

```text
reuse
->
possible accumulation
```

```text
accumulation
->
access to transitions unavailable from an unstructured starting point
```

No mystical source, intention, or universal drive is required.

---

## 3. Large Organizations Are Not Single-Step Outcomes

Physical dynamics constrain how systems can change.

A human body does not appear through a direct transition from a single electron.

A scientific institution does not appear through a direct transition from an isolated molecule.

A trained control system does not appear through a direct transition from random parameters.

Instead:

```text
prior organization
->
intermediate organization
->
further organization
->
current organization
```

The relevant transitions are local, mediated, and path-dependent.

This means that higher-order organization depends on the continued availability of sufficiently organized intermediate states.

If every intermediate state disappears before it can participate in later transformations, the larger organization cannot be assembled.

---

## 4. Accumulation Is Constrained Path Dependence

Accumulation is not arbitrary addition.

It is the preservation of selected consequences of previous transitions so that later transitions can build on them.

Examples include:

```text
stored food
```

```text
learned sensorimotor mappings
```

```text
terrain knowledge
```

```text
roads
```

```text
libraries
```

```text
proofs
```

```text
trained model weights
```

```text
institutional procedures
```

```text
biological inheritance
```

Each carries forward organization that would otherwise need to be reconstructed.

Accumulation is therefore:

> The retention of task-relevant structure across time such that future transitions can begin from a more organized state than would otherwise be available.

---

## 5. Persistence Is Not Exact Microscopic Identity

A maintained organization does not need to preserve every microscopic component.

Bodies replace molecules.

Institutions replace members.

Computers overwrite memory cells.

Roads are repaired with new material.

What persists is usually a functionally sufficient equivalence class.

Let:

\[
X
\]

be the physical state space and:

\[
G \subseteq X
\]

be the set of states that realize the relevant organization.

The long-horizon condition is not:

\[
x_H = x_0
\]

It is:

\[
x_H \in G
\]

The organization persists when the system remains within, or repeatedly returns to, a viable region of state space.

---

## 6. Reconstruction and Maintenance

There are two broad ways to obtain a useful organization at a later time.

### Reconstruction

```text
organization is lost
->
problem is solved again
->
organization is rebuilt
```

### Maintenance

```text
organization persists sufficiently
->
damage is repaired
->
future use begins from retained structure
```

Let:

\[
C_{\text{reconstruct}}
\]

be the expected cost of rebuilding an organization and:

\[
C_{\text{maintain}}
\]

be the expected cost of preserving it over the relevant interval.

Persistence is economically favorable when:

\[
C_{\text{maintain}}
<
C_{\text{reconstruct}}
\]

This does not imply that every system minimizes reconstruction cost.

It implies that scalable organization becomes difficult or impossible when reconstruction repeatedly consumes the resources needed for new activity.

---

## 7. Scale Requires Amortization

Suppose a recurring problem costs:

\[
C_{\text{raw}}
\]

when solved from scratch.

Suppose persistent infrastructure costs:

\[
C_{\text{build}}
\]

to construct,

\[
C_{\text{maintain}}
\]

to preserve,

and:

\[
C_{\text{use}}
\]

per later use.

After \(N\) uses, persistence is beneficial when:

\[
C_{\text{build}}
+
C_{\text{maintain}}
+
N C_{\text{use}}
<
N C_{\text{raw}}
\]

The benefit is not merely that each use becomes cheaper.

The deeper consequence is that saved resources become available for other inverse problems.

This creates room for:

```text
specialization
exploration
coordination
innovation
longer planning horizons
higher organizational complexity
```

---

## 8. Organizational Capacity

A system has finite capacity for sensing, computation, repair, control, and coordination.

Let:

\[
B
\]

be its available resource budget over an interval.

Let:

\[
R
\]

be the cost of restoring previously acquired organization.

Then the capacity remaining for new activity is:

\[
N = B - R
\]

If:

\[
R \approx B
\]

then:

\[
N \approx 0
\]

The system may remain occupied with reconstructing yesterday's capabilities.

It has little capacity for new problems.

This suggests a basic condition for accumulation:

> The recurring cost of preserving or restoring prior competence must remain sufficiently below the total available capacity.

---

## 9. The Restoration Burden

Define the restoration burden:

\[
\rho
=
\frac{C_{\text{restore}}}{B}
\]

where:

```text
C_restore
=
resources needed to recover prior viable organization
```

and:

```text
B
=
total resources available during the interval
```

Then:

```text
rho << 1
->
most capacity remains available for new activity
```

```text
rho approximately 1
->
nearly all capacity is consumed by restoration
```

```text
rho > 1
->
prior organization cannot be restored within the available budget
```

A system can scale only if its restoration burden remains bounded below the level that consumes all available capacity.

---

## 10. Why Stable Control Mappings Matter

Consider a body whose control mappings are randomized during sleep.

A signal that previously moved an arm may later:

```text
move the nose
move an entire region
produce no movement
trigger an unavailable transition
```

The organism must then relearn the mapping from control signals to bodily effects.

If the mapping is randomized every night, learning cannot accumulate across days.

Daily life becomes:

```text
wake
->
identify current body mapping
->
relearn basic control
->
use remaining time
->
sleep
->
lose mapping
```

Even if relearning is physically possible, long-horizon competence collapses when basic reconstruction consumes most of the available interval.

Stable control is therefore not a decorative optimization.

It is infrastructure that allows attention to move beyond immediate self-reconstruction.

---

## 11. Pure Reactivity and Retained State

A system with no retained state can still react.

It may:

```text
avoid an obstacle
follow a gradient
respond to light
withdraw from damage
```

But without persistent internal or external organization, it cannot reliably:

```text
recognize a previously visited place
remember a hidden hazard
exploit a learned shortcut
coordinate a long action sequence
compare current conditions with remote past conditions
```

In navigation:

```text
reactive control
=
act from current observation
```

```text
SLAM-like capability
=
retain and update a map while locating oneself within it
```

The map is accumulated organization.

Without it, the system repeatedly confronts terrain as if it were new.

## 12. Inverse Problems Are Widespread

A forward problem asks:

```text
given mechanism
->
what outcome follows?
```

An inverse problem asks:

```text
given desired outcome
->
what realizable mechanism can produce it?
```

Intentional activity repeatedly creates inverse problems:

```text
reach destination
->
find route and controls
```

```text
obtain food
->
find, produce, store, or exchange resources
```

```text
prove theorem
->
find a valid derivation
```

```text
build software
->
find an implementation
```

```text
coordinate a society
->
find stable procedures and interfaces
```

The same family of inverse problems often recurs.

This recurrence creates pressure for persistent intermediate organization.

## 13. Why Policies Exist

A policy is not fundamental merely because it is called a policy.

Its use is economic and structural.

Without a policy:

```text
observation
->
reconstruct decision process
->
select action
```

With a policy:

```text
observation
+
retained organization
->
action
```

A policy preserves results of prior search, learning, or design.

It allows a current decision to reuse earlier work.

The same applies to:

```text
strategies
proofs
protocols
controllers
libraries
maps
```

They differ in form, but each reduces the need to reconstruct a useful transition structure from scratch.

---

## 14. Proofs as Accumulated Organization

A proof is not only a sequence that establishes a conclusion.

It is preserved organization in reasoning space.

Without retained proofs:

```text
each theorem
must be rediscovered from earlier assumptions
whenever it is used
```

With retained proofs:

```text
validated result
->
reusable intermediate state
->
new derivation
```

A theorem becomes a stable node from which later reasoning can proceed.

Mathematics scales because prior derivations do not need to be repeated in full for every later argument.

---

## 15. Stored Resources as Temporal Bridges

Food storage converts:

```text
past surplus
```

into:

```text
future viability
```

An organism that never stores food may survive in a stable environment with continuous access to resources.

But disturbances introduce periods when immediate acquisition fails.

If resource availability has nonzero variance, then storage reduces sensitivity to temporary shortages.

Let:

\[
I_t
\]

be resource intake,

\[
D_t
\]

be resource demand,

and:

\[
S_t
\]

be stored resources.

Then:

\[
S_{t+1}
=
S_t + I_t - D_t
\]

subject to storage limits and losses.

Without storage:

\[
S_t = 0
\]

and viability may require:

\[
I_t \geq D_t
\]

at every interval.

With storage, temporary deficits can be absorbed:

\[
I_t < D_t
\]

provided prior surplus remains available.

Accumulation therefore creates temporal reach.

---

## 16. Disturbance Accumulates Unless Countered

Persistent organizations exist in environments containing:

```text
noise
wear
decay
resource fluctuation
other agents
measurement error
structural damage
model drift
```

Suppose an organization has a positive uncompensated failure hazard:

\[
\Pr(
\text{failure in next interval}
\mid
\text{currently viable}
)
\geq
\delta
>
0
\]

Then:

\[
\Pr(
\text{viable at horizon } H
)
\leq
(1-\delta)^H
\]

Therefore:

\[
\lim_{H\to\infty}
\Pr(
\text{viable at } H
)
=
0
\]

A fixed positive uncompensated failure rate destroys indefinite long-horizon viability.

---

## 17. A Component Survival Model

Suppose an organization requires \(n\) relations or components.

Let each required component survive one interval with probability:

\[
q<1
\]

Assume, for the simplest model, that survival events are independent and no repair occurs.

Then:

\[
\Pr(
\text{organization survives to }H
)
=
q^{nH}
\]

and:

\[
\lim_{H\to\infty}q^{nH}=0
\]

Independence is not essential to the general conclusion.

The essential condition is persistent positive failure risk without compensating restoration.

---

## 18. Growing Organizational Requirements

The probability problem becomes even stronger when the organization required at horizon \(H\) grows in complexity.

Let:

\[
N(H)
\]

be the number of coordinated constraints that must be satisfied.

Suppose that without accumulation each constraint must be rediscovered, and each has probability at most:

\[
p<1
\]

of being satisfied during fresh reconstruction.

Then:

\[
\Pr(
\text{required organization at } H
)
\leq
p^{N(H)}
\]

If:

\[
N(H)\to\infty
\]

then:

\[
p^{N(H)}\to 0
\]

Thus the probability of repeatedly reconstructing a growing organization from an insufficiently structured state approaches zero.

---

## 19. Accumulation Converts Global Construction Into Local Extension

Without accumulation:

```text
required organization at H
->
reconstruct all necessary structure
```

With accumulation:

```text
organization at H-1
+
local extension
+
repair
->
organization at H
```

Let:

\[
O_H
\]

be the organization available at horizon \(H\).

Accumulation allows:

\[
O_H
=
O_{H-1}
+
\Delta O_H
-
L_H
+
R_H
\]

where:

```text
Delta O_H
=
newly constructed organization
```

```text
L_H
=
organization lost to disturbance
```

```text
R_H
=
organization restored through repair
```

The system need not reconstruct all of \(O_H\).

It needs to preserve enough of \(O_{H-1}\) and produce a manageable increment.

This is the main scaling advantage.

---

## 20. Long-Horizon Viability

Long-horizon viability is not merely surviving for a long chronological duration.

It means remaining capable of supporting transitions whose dependencies extend across many intervals.

A system has a longer effective horizon when it can preserve:

```text
resources
state estimates
control mappings
learned distinctions
partial constructions
commitments
external infrastructure
```

long enough for them to affect later outcomes.

The effective horizon is therefore partly determined by memory and persistence.

A system may exist for years while remaining behaviorally short-horizon if useful organization repeatedly decays before later actions can depend on it.

---

## 21. A Viability Condition

Let:

\[
D(H)
\]

be cumulative disturbance by horizon \(H\),

\[
R(H)
\]

be cumulative retained corrective capacity,

and:

\[
B(H)
\]

be the tolerance of the viable organization.

A necessary condition for viability is:

\[
D(H)-R(H)
\leq
B(H)
\]

If:

\[
D(H)\sim dH,
\qquad d>0
\]

while:

\[
R(H)=o(H)
\]

and tolerance does not grow comparably, then eventually:

\[
D(H)-R(H)>B(H)
\]

Under ordinary concentration assumptions:

\[
\Pr(
\text{viable at }H
)
\to
0
\]

This yields a general statement:

> In an environment with persistent disturbance, a bounded organization whose corrective capacity grows more slowly than cumulative disturbance cannot remain viable with nonvanishing probability over arbitrarily long horizons.

---

## 22. Maintenance as Active Persistence

Persistence is not always passive durability.

It often requires active processes:

```text
repair
error correction
replacement
recalibration
rehearsal
retraining
reindexing
institutional revision
```

A maintained structure may change continuously while preserving its functional organization.

Therefore:

```text
persistence
is not
absence of change
```

It is:

```text
change constrained so that viability is preserved
```

---

## 23. Repair Changes the Probability Law

Suppose component failures occur at rate:

\[
\lambda
\]

and repair occurs at rate:

\[
\mu
\]

Without repair:

```text
failures accumulate
->
probability of viability decays
```

With sufficient repair:

```text
failures occur
->
repair returns system toward viable region
->
stationary or recurrent viability may become possible
```

The key comparison is not simply whether failure exists.

It is whether corrective processes can keep pace with disturbance.

In rough terms:

```text
repair capacity > effective damage rate
->
long-lived viability may be possible
```

```text
repair capacity < effective damage rate
->
viability probability decays
```

## 24. Accumulation Need Not Be Internal

A system can accumulate organization internally or externally.

### Internal accumulation

```text
memory
learned weights
stable morphology
metabolic reserves
internal models
```

### External accumulation

```text
tools
roads
books
databases
buildings
institutions
stored food
```

External organization can reduce the burden on internal memory and control.

A road carries prior traversal and engineering effort outside the traveler.

A book carries prior reasoning outside the reader.

A market carries coordination procedures outside any one participant.

Accumulation is therefore distributed across bodies, artifacts, and institutions.

## 25. Specialization Depends on Released Capacity

Suppose every person must spend nearly all available time producing immediate subsistence.

Then little capacity remains for:

```text
science
medicine
engineering
art
administration
streaming
entertainment
specialized craft
```

Specialization becomes possible when persistent organization reduces recurring costs.

Examples include:

```text
agriculture
storage
transportation
energy infrastructure
legal coordination
currency
communication networks
```

These do not create specialization through intention alone.

They release capacity by lowering the cost of repeatedly solving basic survival and coordination problems.

Therefore:

> Specialization is enabled when accumulated organization reduces recurring reconstruction and subsistence burdens enough to free resources for narrower problem families.

## 26. Accumulation and Attention

Attention is finite.

A system occupied with maintaining basic control cannot allocate the same resources to remote or abstract problems.

If basic mappings are unstable, attention is consumed by:

```text
Where is my body?
What does this control do?
Which path still exists?
Which distinctions remain valid?
```

Stable organization allows attention to move upward:

```text
basic control
->
coordination
->
planning
->
exploration
->
abstract reasoning
```

This is not a hierarchy of intrinsic value.

It is a hierarchy of dependencies.

Higher-order tasks often presuppose that lower-order organization does not need to be reconstructed continuously.

## 27. Multidimensional Cost

Reconstruction and maintenance costs are not one-dimensional.

Let the cost vector be:

\[
\mathbf C
=
(
C_E,
C_T,
C_M,
C_R,
C_A,
C_K,
C_U,
\dots
)
\]

where, for example:

```text
C_E = energy
C_T = time
C_M = material
C_R = risk
C_A = attention
C_K = coordination
C_U = uncertainty
```

Different systems operate under different bottlenecks.

A spacecraft may be dominated by energy and mass.

A nervous system may be dominated by metabolic cost, latency, and error tolerance.

A legal institution may be dominated by verification and coordination.

A network service may be dominated by latency, bandwidth, and failure recovery.

---

## 28. Dominant Cost Modes

Although the full cost space may be high-dimensional, not all directions matter equally at every scale.

A small number of dominant modes may account for most practical constraint.

This is analogous to:

```text
Taylor approximation
->
retain terms that dominate in a selected regime
```

and:

```text
principal component analysis
->
retain directions explaining disproportionate variation
```

The analogy is structural rather than exact.

At a selected timescale and operating regime, a few costs may dominate the viability calculation.

For example:

```text
milliseconds
->
latency and control stability
```

```text
hours
->
energy and attention
```

```text
years
->
maintenance and adaptation
```

```text
decades
->
replacement, institutional continuity, and accumulated error
```

A useful model should identify which cost components dominate at the horizon being studied.

## 29. Timescale-Dependent Weights

Let:

\[
C_i(H)
\]

be cost component \(i\) evaluated over horizon \(H\).

Let:

\[
w_i(H)
\]

be its horizon-dependent importance.

Then an effective cost may be represented as:

\[
J(H)
=
\sum_i
w_i(H)C_i(H)
\]

The weights need not be constant.

A structure can be expensive at short horizons and beneficial at long horizons.

For example:

```text
building a road
=
high immediate construction cost
+
low repeated traversal cost
```

```text
learning a control policy
=
high initial search cost
+
low repeated decision cost
```

```text
proving a theorem
=
high initial reasoning cost
+
low repeated reuse cost
```

The correct comparison depends on expected reuse and horizon.

## 30. Break-Even Horizon

Suppose maintaining or constructing infrastructure costs:

\[
C_0
\]

and saves:

\[
s
\]

per unit time or per use.

Then a simple break-even horizon is:

\[
H^*
=
\frac{C_0}{s}
\]

For:

\[
H < H^*
\]

the infrastructure may appear wasteful.

For:

\[
H > H^*
\]

its accumulated savings dominate the initial cost.

This explains why short-horizon systems may favor reaction, while long-horizon systems favor memory, maintenance, and infrastructure.

## 31. Accumulation and Optionality

Persistent organization does not only preserve one path.

It can preserve a region of future possibilities.

Stored energy can support several later actions.

A general-purpose tool can solve several later problems.

A map can support many routes.

A scientific theory can support many predictions.

Therefore the value of accumulation may be measured not only by one realized outcome, but by preserved optionality.

Let:

\[
\Omega(R,H)
\]

be the set of viable future transitions supported by retained organization \(R\) at horizon \(H\).

A structure may be valuable when it increases:

\[
|\Omega(R,H)|
\]

or improves the quality of paths within that set.

---

## 32. Accumulation and Sufficient Paths

Accumulated organization supports sufficient paths by retaining selected connectivity.

A map does not preserve every physical distinction.

A policy does not enumerate every possible action sequence.

A proof does not repeat every foundational derivation.

A library does not expose every machine-level transition.

Instead:

```text
irrelevant distinctions are suppressed
+
useful transitions are preserved
->
sufficiently cheap future traversal
```

Accumulation therefore depends on selective preservation.

Preserving everything would itself be too costly.

---

## 33. Accumulation Can Fail Through Rigidity

Persistent organization is not automatically beneficial.

It can become maladaptive when:

```text
the environment changes
```

```text
the retained mapping becomes false
```

```text
the cost of maintenance exceeds reconstruction
```

```text
the infrastructure blocks superior alternatives
```

```text
the preserved distinction is no longer relevant
```

```text
the organization monopolizes access to important paths
```

Thus a viable accumulation mechanism must support both:

```text
retention
and
revision
```

The problem is not to maximize persistence.

It is to preserve useful organization without preventing necessary reconstruction.

---

## 34. Forgetting and Decomposition

Accumulation requires forgetting as well as retention.

If obsolete structure is never removed, maintenance burden can grow without bound.

A system may need to:

```text
delete stale information
prune unused connections
retire institutions
replace failed components
compress redundant records
abandon invalid models
```

Therefore:

```text
accumulation
is not
monotonic storage of everything
```

It is:

```text
selective preservation
+
selective removal
+
continuous reorganization
```

## 35. A General Value Functional

Let \(R\) be a persistent organization supporting a future query distribution \(Q_H\) over horizon \(H\).

A general value functional may be written as:

\[
V(R,H)
=
\mathbb E_{q\sim Q_H}
[
U(q,R)
]
-
C_{\text{build}}(R)
-
C_{\text{maintain}}(R,H)
-
C_{\text{use}}(R,H)
-
C_{\text{rigidity}}(R,H)
\]

where:

```text
U(q,R)
=
utility from solving query q with retained organization R
```

```text
C_build
=
construction cost
```

```text
C_maintain
=
preservation and repair cost
```

```text
C_use
=
query-time or execution cost
```

```text
C_rigidity
=
cost of being constrained by obsolete or overly narrow organization
```

This functional is multidimensional in practice.

The scalar form represents a selected projection for a particular organization and horizon.

## 36. A Long-Horizon Accumulation Criterion

Let:

\[
G_H
\]

be the viable organization required at horizon \(H\).

Let:

\[
P_0(G_H)
\]

be the probability of realizing it without retained organization.

Let:

\[
P_R(G_H)
\]

be the probability of realizing it with accumulation mechanism \(R\).

A useful accumulation mechanism satisfies:

\[
P_R(G_H)
\gg
P_0(G_H)
\]

for the relevant horizon and cost envelope.

For scaling systems, the stronger requirement is that:

\[
\liminf_{H\to\infty}P_R(G_H)>0
\]

or at least that viability decays much more slowly than without accumulation.

If:

\[
P_0(G_H)\to 0
\]

while:

\[
P_R(G_H)
\]

remains bounded away from zero over the supported horizon, then accumulated organization is not merely convenient.

It is what makes the long-horizon organization reachable with non-negligible probability.

---

## 37. A Scaling Condition

Suppose the organization required at horizon \(H\) has construction complexity:

\[
N(H)
\]

Without accumulation, all \(N(H)\) components must be established during the current construction interval.

With accumulation, only an increment:

\[
\Delta N(H)
=
N(H)-N(H-1)
\]

plus repair must be produced.

Scalable accumulation requires that the expected cost of extension and repair grow more slowly than full reconstruction:

\[
C_{\text{extend}}(H)
+
C_{\text{repair}}(H)
=
 o(
C_{\text{reconstruct}}(H)
)
\]

in the regime of interest.

This asymptotic separation is what allows organizational depth to increase.

## 38. Layers of Accumulated Organization

Complex systems often contain nested persistence layers.

For example:

```text
stable physical substrate
->
components
->
control mappings
->
learned policies
->
tools
->
institutions
->
shared knowledge
```

Each layer reduces reconstruction burden for the next.

A failure at a lower layer can invalidate many higher layers.

Therefore long-horizon systems require not only accumulation, but dependency-aware maintenance.

The value of a layer depends partly on how much higher-order organization relies on it.

---

## 39. Cascading Reconstruction Cost

Suppose layer \(L_k\) depends on \(L_{k-1}\).

If \(L_{k-1}\) is lost, reconstructing \(L_k\) may also become necessary.

Therefore the cost of lower-layer failure may be:

\[
C_{\text{failure}}(L_{k-1})
=
C_{\text{repair}}(L_{k-1})
+
\sum_{j=k}^{m}
C_{\text{reconstruct}}(L_j)
\]

This explains why foundational infrastructure may receive disproportionate protection.

Stable power, memory, control, food supply, and communication can support many dependent activities.

Their failure has multiplicative rather than merely local effects.

---

## 40. Long Horizons and Probability Collapse

The phrase "long horizon" can now be unpacked.

A longer horizon means:

```text
more opportunities for disturbance
```

```text
more dependencies that must remain valid
```

```text
more intermediate organization that must persist
```

```text
more chances for reconstruction burden to exceed capacity
```

Without accumulation and repair, survival probabilities multiply downward.

With growing organizational requirements, fresh reconstruction probabilities may shrink exponentially.

Thus:

> The probability of a nontrivial organization at horizon \(H\) approaches zero when disturbance remains positive, corrective organization does not accumulate sufficiently, and the required organization grows faster than it can be reconstructed within the available budget.

## 41. Core Construction

The framework can be summarized as:

```text
physical transitions are local and constrained
->
```

## 42. Core Principles

### Principle 1: Large organization requires intermediate organization

Complex outcomes are reached through constrained physical transitions rather than arbitrary jumps.

### Principle 2: Persistence makes reuse possible

A later process can build on earlier work only if enough of that work remains available.

### Principle 3: Accumulation is retained task-relevant organization

It carries selected consequences of past transitions into future ones.

### Principle 4: Functional persistence does not require microscopic identity

Organizations may replace components while remaining within a viable equivalence class.

### Principle 5: Repeated reconstruction consumes organizational capacity

A system occupied with restoring prior competence has less capacity for new problems.

### Principle 6: Scale requires amortization

Recurring inverse problems become scalable when construction cost is distributed across many future uses.

### Principle 7: Long horizons multiply disturbance opportunities

Without corrective accumulation, viability probabilities tend to decay.

### Principle 8: Growing complexity makes fresh reconstruction improbable

When the number of coordinated constraints grows, rebuilding all of them from scratch becomes increasingly unlikely or costly.

### Principle 9: Accumulation converts global construction into local extension

Preserved organization allows later systems to add increments rather than rebuild wholes.

### Principle 10: Maintenance is active constrained change

Persistence often requires repair, replacement, error correction, and recalibration.

### Principle 11: Accumulation is distributed

Useful organization may be stored in bodies, tools, environments, records, and institutions.

### Principle 12: Specialization depends on released capacity

Persistent infrastructure lowers recurring burdens and makes narrower activity possible.

### Principle 13: Cost is multidimensional and horizon-dependent

Energy, time, risk, attention, material, and coordination matter differently across regimes.

### Principle 14: Dominant cost modes can change with timescale

Short-horizon and long-horizon viability may be limited by different components.

### Principle 15: Persistence must remain revisable

Retained organization can become rigid, stale, or harmful when conditions change.

### Principle 16: Forgetting is part of accumulation

Selective removal prevents obsolete structure from consuming unbounded maintenance capacity.

### Principle 17: Foundational failures create cascading reconstruction

Lower-layer breakdown can invalidate many dependent organizations.

### Principle 18: Long-horizon viability requires corrective capacity that scales with disturbance

If disturbance grows faster than retained repair and tolerance, viability probability approaches zero.

## 44. Design Target

The design target is a framework in which:

```text
no teleological or anthropomorphic principle is required
```

```text
large organizations are treated as products
of constrained intermediate transitions
```

```text
accumulation is defined as retained task-relevant organization
```

```text
maintenance and reconstruction are explicitly compared
```

```text
finite organizational capacity limits repeated restoration
```

```text
long horizons are analyzed as repeated exposure
to disturbance and dependency failure
```

```text
probability of viability can be bounded
under uncompensated hazard
```

```text
growing complexity is separated from mere duration
```

```text
cost is treated as multidimensional
and dependent on regime and timescale
```

```text
specialization is explained through released capacity
rather than assumed as a primitive goal
```

```text
persistence, repair, forgetting, and revision
are analyzed as parts of one process
```

The central claim is:

> Complex long-horizon organization does not require a mystical drive toward progress. It requires that reality permit sufficiently organized intermediate states to persist, influence later transitions, and be repaired or extended at lower cost than full reconstruction. Without such accumulation, persistent disturbance creates repeated opportunities for failure, growing organizations must be rebuilt from increasingly improbable combinations, and restoration consumes the capacity needed for new activity. Accumulation changes the structure of the problem: instead of constructing the whole organization again, the system preserves prior structure and pays mainly for local extension, adaptation, and repair. This makes longer effective horizons, specialization, optionality, and increasing organizational depth physically and economically possible.

Reality does not need to seek accumulation.

It only needs to permit retained organization to affect what can happen next.

A body does not need to relearn itself every morning.

A map does not need to rediscover every road for every trip.

A proof does not need to be repeated from first principles whenever it is used.

A civilization does not need to reconstruct all of subsistence before every specialized act.

What matters is whether enough organization survives, adapts, and remains affordable to maintain so that future transitions can begin from more than an effectively unstructured state.
